The narrative and concept

When help
takes over.

The intended experience: a shadow will teach me to play. Each time I borrow its power, winning should feel easier. Eventually, it will start treating my next move as its own.

Shadow Pact is a proposal for a single-player browser game built around one main interaction: choosing and playing cards. Random rewards and relic combinations are intended to make each run different. The planned scope is five encounters, with choices about accepting help and building alternatives. The target is a short run of about five to ten minutes, to be checked during Week 3 playtesting. This Week 1 page presents research, proposed rules, and concept studies; building the playable game belongs to Week 2.

Why this subject is personal

I decided to quit smoking when I noticed that I could not keep up physically with other people my age. I chose this subject because of my own struggle with addiction and the illusions surrounding it: believing I needed cigarettes to feel ready or in control. The second day was hardest for me; that memory informs the pressure of the game’s midpoint.

My interpretation of Hallucination

I use Hallucination as a metaphor for the illusion that I need something in order to cope. The shadow offers that reassurance: borrowed power makes the table brighter and winning easier, while gradually taking over my decisions. Regaining control means testing that belief through action. A simple card table and opponents such as Habit and Another Me make this internal struggle visible.

I want the player to move from curiosity to enjoyment, then notice reliance and try a different way of acting. Recovery includes learning, preparation, and support. Asking for help again does not erase the run. The story draws on my experience, rather than prescribing a universal timeline or treating a difficult moment as a moral failure.

Inscryption gives me the ambition: strong narrative and a desire to try another combination. Scoundrel provides a compact browser comparison.

My personal reference: Inscryption

I am drawn to the way it breaks the fourth wall, changes visual worlds and map styles, and connects addictive card play with an unfolding story. These are the parts I remember most; I want the story in my project to grow from the player’s changing relationship with the rules.

The official listing identifies Daniel Mullins Games as developer and Devolver Digital as publisher, and describes a combination of deckbuilding roguelike, escape-room puzzles, and psychological horror. The official soundtrack credits composer Jonah Senzel. These are verified credits; my reading of trust and dependence is my interpretation, not a claim about the creators’ stated intention. Browser case: Scoundrel

A single-player card dungeon: resolve monsters, collect weapons and use potions while preserving health. Four visible cards offer local choices; resolving three advances to another room while one card carries over. The studied web version uses directional controls and a confirm button. The original card game is by Zach Gage and Kurt Bieg (2011); the browser adaptation studied here is by Benjamin Brown. Credits for the adaptation and original design are kept separate. What I am taking forward

A small interface can support meaningful choices when timing and future consequences matter. The weapon preview makes an immediate cost legible even when later encounters remain random. For Shadow Pact, the shadow’s offer should be genuinely useful, and its dependence cost should be visible before accepting. Randomness should create new combinations while keeping each immediate decision understandable.

The critical discussion with AI

I wanted
a game first.

My corrections changed both the scope and the meaning of the project.

Gap 1: atmosphere was standing in for play

AI initially emphasized mysterious rooms, visual transformation, and choosing an exit. That did not explain what would make the next run enjoyable. I returned to what I liked in Inscryption: card and item combinations, randomness, and narrative. The design decision was to make card combat and reward selection the repeated activity.

Gap 2: the metaphor was expanding the scope

An elaborate setting added locations, equipment, and a story structure before the core interaction worked. I challenged the complexity and proposed a simpler visual change: sober colors becoming vivid after accepting an aid. The final direction keeps one table, a small card set, and the shadow relationship.

A source check that changed the research

The lesson specifically asks for a browser-game case. Inscryption is my personal reference, but I should not present it as a browser game. A documented Scoundrel browser session now provides direct action-response evidence. The official game listing supports Inscryption’s credits and genre; it does not establish that its creators intended an addiction metaphor.

My conclusion

A small interface can still contain strategic depth. I chose a card roguelike so the player can enjoy discovering a build; the metaphor lives in how an attractive assistant changes that play. AI was useful for alternatives, research organization, and draft interface visuals. I retained responsibility for the subject, rejected directions that did not fit, and used evidence to separate observed behavior from interpretation.

The interaction and player journey

Learn.
Rely.
Reclaim.

Proposed journey: one main interaction will develop across three short stages, with an opening and an ending.

The planned five encounters are grouped into three narrative stages. Assistance will remain optional throughout, with an independent route to be built and tested. The sequence below describes the intended experience and proposed rules, rather than a finished game or a required sequence of choices.

  1. Opening: meet the shadow

    Enter a quiet table and inspect the starter cards. The shadow explains enemy intent and energy. Clicking Start begins the first fight.

  2. Stage 1: learn / encounters 1–2

    Read an attack, block it, and choose when to deal damage. A first victory introduces random cards and relics. The player learns that order and combinations matter.

  3. Stage 2: rely / encounter 3, “the second day”

    A stronger opponent pressures the developing build. Help offers immediate energy, a card, and vivid color. Repeated use raises dependence. At three dependence, an initial rule to explore would let the shadow play an affordable remaining card when End Turn is pressed. The personal memory of my second day informs this midpoint, not a universal withdrawal schedule.

  4. Stage 3: reclaim / encounters 4–5

    Try combinations that replace borrowed power. Two consecutive turns without help reduce dependence. The final opponent, Another Me, tests the build and the player’s decisions.

  5. Ending: whose move is it?

    At zero dependence, the shadow returns to following. An assisted victory also reaches the exit and leaves room for another attempt. The planned loss screen will offer a new deal or another attempt with the same random seed. The closing sentence connects the conclusion to my own life.

The style and sound guide

The same table.
A different pull.

Temptation changes the atmosphere. Information stays readable.

The proposal follows my existing GINTER STUDIO project-page format: black ground, Poppins typography, large titles, thin rules, and spacious images. Inside the game, charcoal and bone-colored cards create a more intimate table. Serif card names contrast with sans-serif instructions and tabular values.

Visual reference: Inscryption’s official presentation is a reference for the relationship between a card table and an unsettling opponent. Scoundrel’s recorded screens are a reference for a compact decision space and explicit outcomes. These inform my choices; their commercial artwork is not copied into the game.

Interface: all player-facing text will be in English. Short labels such as Play, Borrow, and End Turn will sit beside clear icons and numbers. Each prompt will introduce one action; selecting a card will reveal extra detail only when needed. Card cost, enemy intent, and the price of help will stay visible. The player should understand the next move at a glance.

Motion: a small card lift confirms selection; a short, subtle tremor can follow the assisted state fading. The game will include a visible motion toggle and respect system reduced-motion settings. A static change and text warning retain the meaning when movement is off.

The two-week build plan

Build a loop.
Test the choice.

Keep the scope at one table, five encounters, and a small set of interacting effects.

Week 2 will build the first playable HTML/CSS/JavaScript version: a small card pool, random relic rewards, five encounters, visible enemy intent, and an assistance/dependence loop. The concept studies on this page communicate the proposed direction. Card counts, balance, pacing, and the strength of the shadow’s intervention will be decided through building and observation.

An anticipated design risk: if intervention happens only after End Turn, it may feel like an extra benefit rather than a loss of agency. During Week 2, I will compare that candidate rule with a clearly announced play-order interruption and a visible way to recover control. Week 3 observations will determine whether the intervention communicates its intended consequence.

Questions for the playtest

Can players identify the next action at a glance and explain enemy intent and card cost without reading a long tutorial? Can they name a combination they discovered? Do they notice the shadow’s price before using it? Does intervention feel like a change in agency? Can they find a viable way to stop borrowing? Do they want another run because of a new strategy, the story, or both?
